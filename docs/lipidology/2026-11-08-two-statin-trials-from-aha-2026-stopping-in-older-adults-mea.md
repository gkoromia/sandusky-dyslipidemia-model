## 1.0 Summary

Two randomized trials presented at the American Heart Association Scientific Sessions 2026 looked at how statins are used, not whether they work. In STREAM, more than 1,880 adults over 70 who had at least two chronic conditions and no known cardiovascular disease were assigned either to stop or to keep taking their statin. Stopping passed the trial's statistical test for being no worse (noninferior), yet over about two years 12.7% of those who stopped had a major cardiovascular event or died, compared with 9.2% of those who continued. The trial's safety monitoring board advised the people who had stopped to talk with their care team about restarting. In eZ-PAVE, 3,048 adults in South Korea with established heart or artery disease started on either a statin plus ezetimibe or a statin alone, and doses were adjusted to reach an LDL cholesterol goal. After three years, event rates were 8.5% and 7.9%, a difference that was not statistically significant. Neither trial changes this site's approach. A statin already prescribed to an older adult should not be stopped by default, and starting with a statin and adding ezetimibe when needed remains reasonable. Both summaries come from society news coverage, not the full journal articles.

## 2.0 Key Points

1. STREAM randomly assigned more than 1,880 adults over 70 (nearly half women), taking a statin for primary prevention and living with at least two chronic conditions, to stop or continue it [1].
2. Stopping was noninferior on two-year restricted mean event-free survival (difference -2.0 weeks), but over a median 103 weeks a primary-outcome event occurred in 119 people (12.7%) who stopped and 87 (9.2%) who continued, and serious adverse events in 43% and 39.8% [1].
3. eZ-PAVE randomly assigned 3,048 adults with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease to start a statin plus ezetimibe or a statin alone, and separately to an LDL goal below 55 or below 70 mg/dL; the three-year composite event rate was 8.5% with the combination and 7.9% with a statin alone, not a significant difference [2].
4. Starting with the combination reached lower average LDL cholesterol (53 vs 62 mg/dL in the lower-goal group, 63 vs 70 mg/dL in the conventional-goal group), but ezetimibe was later added for more than 34% and nearly 25% of those who started on a statin alone [1, 2].
5. eZ-PAVE was open-label, was run only in South Korea with 79.1% men, and was not sized specifically to compare the two starting strategies [2].

## 3.0 Stopping statins in older adults: STREAM

Primary prevention means taking a statin to prevent a first heart attack or stroke rather than a repeat one. STREAM tested what happens when older adults with several health problems stop a statin taken for that purpose.

The international trial enrolled more than 1,880 people over 70, nearly half of them women. All were taking a statin for primary prevention and had at least two chronic conditions. They were randomly assigned to stop or to continue the statin [1].

The trial asked whether stopping was noninferior, meaning not unacceptably worse than continuing. It measured this with restricted mean event-free survival time, which is the average time people went without a major cardiovascular event or death over two years. The difference was 2.0 weeks in favor of continuing, which met the trial's test for noninferiority [1].

The event counts tell a less comfortable story. Over a median follow-up of 103 weeks, 119 people who stopped (12.7%) and 87 who continued (9.2%) had a major cardiovascular event or died from any cause. At least one serious adverse event occurred in 43% of those who stopped and 39.8% of those who continued [1].

The lead investigator, Nicolas Rodondi, said the increase in cardiovascular events after stopping means the decision should be shared between patient and clinician. The trial's data and safety monitoring board recommended that people assigned to stop be advised to discuss restarting their statin with their care team [1].

STREAM looks at the opposite question from STAREE, which tested starting atorvastatin in healthy adults aged 70 or older and was covered in an [earlier post]({% link lipidology/2026-09-14-a-statin-cut-heart-attacks-in-healthy-people-over-70-but-did.md %}). STREAM's participants were already on a statin and had more health problems.

## 4.0 Starting with one drug or two: eZ-PAVE

Ezetimibe is a non-statin drug that lowers LDL cholesterol by reducing how much cholesterol the body absorbs from food and how much it makes. It is often the first drug added when a statin alone does not bring LDL cholesterol low enough [2]. eZ-PAVE asked whether starting both drugs at once would prevent more heart attacks and strokes than starting with a statin and adding ezetimibe later.

The trial enrolled 3,048 adults with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, meaning heart or artery disease caused by plaque, at 17 hospitals in South Korea between January 2021 and July 2022. Their average age was 64.4 years and 79.1% were men [2]. It used a 2-by-2 factorial design, meaning each person was randomly assigned twice: once to a starting treatment (statin plus ezetimibe, 1,525 people, or statin alone, 1,523 people) and once to an LDL goal (below 55 mg/dL or below 70 mg/dL) [2].

Doctors adjusted treatment in both groups at each visit, following a set protocol, to reach the assigned goal. Changes were allowed in both directions, from one drug to two and from two to one, and everyone was analyzed in the group they were first assigned to [2]. The starting-treatment comparison was published in Circulation as a prespecified secondary analysis of the trial [3].

The combination group reached lower LDL cholesterol. In the lower-goal group, average LDL was 53 mg/dL with the combination and 62 mg/dL with a statin alone. In the conventional-goal group, it was 63 mg/dL and 70 mg/dL [1, 2]. Ezetimibe was eventually added for more than 34% of the lower-goal patients and nearly 25% of the conventional-goal patients who started on a statin alone [2].

After three years, the combined rate of cardiovascular death, heart attack, stroke, procedures to reopen arteries, and hospitalization for worsening chest pain was 8.5% with the combination and 7.9% with a statin alone. That difference was not statistically significant [2]. The benefit of the lower LDL goal, reported earlier from this trial, was similar whatever the starting treatment, and side effects were similar between the two starting strategies [2].

The lead investigator, Byeong-Keuk Kim, said the team had expected the combination to prevent more events. He contrasted the result with an earlier large trial in the New England Journal of Medicine in which adding ezetimibe to a statin did reduce events, noting that the earlier trial was not designed to adjust treatment to an LDL goal. In his view, the difference is that eZ-PAVE adjusted doses in both groups to reach the target [1, 2]. He said starting with a statin and adding ezetimibe as needed seems a reasonable approach for stable disease when cholesterol is checked regularly, and that starting with both drugs lowers LDL faster and may be practical when LDL is far above goal [2].

## 5.0 Where it fits in practice

eZ-PAVE fits the stepwise approach this site already uses. The model starts with the highest statin dose the patient tolerates, rechecks lipids, and adds ezetimibe if LDL cholesterol or apolipoprotein B is not at target ({% link clinical/05-treatment-pathways.md %}). The trial suggests that, when lipids really are rechecked and treatment adjusted, this approach reaches the same three-year event rates as starting with two drugs. The recheck is what makes it work, and the model's schedule for it is in {% link clinical/12-follow-up-protocol.md %}.

This does not conflict with the imaging result in our [recent post on FLOWPROMOTE]({% link lipidology/2026-11-01-a-stronger-statin-plus-ezetimibe-shrank-coronary-plaque-more.md %}), where a stronger statin plus ezetimibe shrank coronary plaque more. Lower LDL still looks better. eZ-PAVE shows only that getting there in one step instead of two made no measurable difference to events over three years.

STREAM argues against stopping a statin in an older adult simply because of age or a long medication list. The extra events after stopping make discontinuation a decision to talk through with the patient, weighing life expectancy, side effects and the patient's own priorities, not a routine clean-up step. How this site estimates cardiovascular risk is set out in {% link clinical/04-risk-stratification.md %}.

## 6.0 Open questions

The STREAM findings here come from American College of Cardiology news coverage of the presentation and the simultaneous New England Journal of Medicine paper, not the paper itself [1]. The summary does not give the noninferiority margin or a confidence interval for the difference in events, so it is not possible to say from this source how much harm the trial's definition of no worse allowed. It also does not say which events drove the difference, which chronic conditions participants had, or what the serious adverse events were. Follow-up was about two years.

eZ-PAVE was open-label, so patients and doctors knew which treatment was assigned. It ran for three years, which may be too short for a difference to appear. All participants were in South Korea and about 4 in 5 were men, so the results may not apply elsewhere. The number of participants was not calculated specifically to compare the two starting treatments, so a small difference could have been missed [2]. Because many statin-alone patients later received ezetimibe, the trial compared two ways of reaching a goal rather than ezetimibe against no ezetimibe, and the gap in average LDL between the starting strategies was only 7 to 9 mg/dL [1, 2]. The study was funded by the Yuhan Corporation and the Cardiovascular Research Center in Seoul [2].

The investigators said more research is needed to identify which patients would benefit most from starting with combination therapy [2].
