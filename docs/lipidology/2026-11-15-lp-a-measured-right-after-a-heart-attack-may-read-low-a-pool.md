## 1.0 Summary

Lipoprotein(a), or Lp(a), is a cholesterol-carrying particle whose blood level is set mostly by genes, so guidelines advise measuring it once in a lifetime. A systematic review and meta-analysis in the European Journal of Preventive Cardiology pooled 16 studies of 1,396 patients who had serial Lp(a) measurements after an acute coronary syndrome (ACS), the umbrella term for heart attacks and unstable angina. Compared with the first reading after the event, Lp(a) was about the same at 24 hours but was 44% higher at 7 to 14 days, 28% higher at one month and 22% higher at three to six months. In two of the studies, about one patient in five had a rise of more than 25 nmol/L by three to six months. The authors suggest repeating the test weeks to months later when the first result is close to a treatment threshold. A separate expert review in Clinical Chemistry gives similar advice. The analysis was labeled exploratory, pooled small observational studies of mixed design, and did not look at outcomes, and this summary is based on abstracts only.

## 2.0 Key Points

1. The meta-analysis included 16 studies with 1,396 patients who had Lp(a) measured at two or more times after an acute coronary syndrome [1].
2. Using the first post-event reading as the reference, the pooled change was +2.5% at 24 hours (95% CI -6.8 to 11.7), +44.0% at 7 to 14 days (95% CI 4.0 to 84.1), +28.3% at 1 month (95% CI 2.7 to 54.0) and +22.2% at 3 to 6 months (95% CI 2.4 to 42.0) [1].
3. In the two studies that reported absolute changes, Lp(a) rose by more than 25 nmol/L in 17% of patients by 3 months and 22% by 6 months [1].
4. The authors conclude that early post-event readings may underestimate long-term Lp(a) and that a repeat test is most useful when the first value is near a decision threshold [1].
5. A Clinical Chemistry review advises a one-time test for all adults, with repeat testing at a suspected heart attack or stroke and again after one month, in some women after menopause, and when Lp(a)-lowering drugs are used [2].

## 3.0 Why timing matters

Lp(a) is a cause of atherosclerotic heart disease and of aortic valve stenosis, a narrowing of the main valve out of the heart. More than 90% of a person's level is genetically determined [2]. Because it changes little over a lifetime, most guidelines now recommend measuring it once in every adult, ideally with the first cholesterol panel, and reporting it in nmol/L [2].

Many people have their first Lp(a) test in the hospital after a heart attack. If an acute coronary syndrome shifts the level for a while, that single test could misclassify someone near a cutoff [1].

## 4.0 Evidence base

The investigators followed PRISMA 2020, the standard guideline for reporting systematic reviews, and searched MEDLINE and Embase through February 2026 for studies that measured Lp(a) at least twice after an acute coronary syndrome. Of 3,811 records, 16 studies with 1,396 patients qualified. They pooled the results with a random-effects meta-analysis, a method that allows for differences between studies, and labeled it exploratory [1].

Each patient's first reading after the event served as the reference. At 24 hours, Lp(a) was essentially unchanged, at +2.5% (95% CI -6.8 to 11.7; P=0.602). It was higher at every later time point: +44.0% at 7 to 14 days (95% CI 4.0 to 84.1; P=0.031), +28.3% at 1 month (95% CI 2.7 to 54.0; P=0.030) and +22.2% at 3 to 6 months (95% CI 2.4 to 42.0; P=0.028) [1]. The confidence intervals are wide, and the lower ends sit close to zero.

Two of the studies reported absolute changes. In them, Lp(a) rose by more than 25 nmol/L in 17% of patients by 3 months and 22% by 6 months [1].

The authors conclude that Lp(a) is not static after an acute coronary syndrome and that early readings may underestimate long-term levels and risk. They suggest a repeat measurement weeks to months later, particularly when the first value is near a clinical decision threshold or would affect eligibility for Lp(a)-lowering drugs in development [1].

## 5.0 Where it fits in practice

The Clinical Chemistry review reaches a similar practical answer. It keeps the one-time test as the default but lists situations where a repeat is reasonable: initially at a suspected acute coronary syndrome or stroke and again after one month, sometimes in women after menopause, and when drugs are given to lower Lp(a) [2]. It defines high Lp(a) as above 105 nmol/L (50 mg/dL) [2]. This site uses 125 nmol/L as its threshold for elevated risk.

The Sandusky model currently measures Lp(a) once in every new patient and repeats it only when a borderline result was obtained on a different assay ({% link clinical/04-risk-stratification.md %}). These findings point to one more case for a repeat: a result drawn during or soon after an acute coronary syndrome that falls near the model's cutoffs. Because the pooled changes after 24 hours were all upward, an early result just below a cutoff is the one most worth repeating [1].

The drugs that might make precise Lp(a) cutoffs more important are not yet proven. The review notes that pelacarsen lowered Lp(a) in the phase 3 Lp(a)HORIZON trial but did not meet its primary cardiovascular endpoint, and that three other outcome trials are still running [2].

## 6.0 Open questions

The meta-analysis pooled 16 observational studies of mixed design and size, and the authors called it exploratory [1]. The abstract does not say which assays and units each study used, what lipid-lowering treatment patients started in the weeks after the event, or how the studies differed in patient characteristics. It reports no clinical outcomes [1].

The pattern also leaves a question the abstract does not answer: whether the higher later values reflect the person's true long-term level or a slow-fading effect of the event and its treatment. Either way, the first in-hospital reading was the lowest one in these data [1].

The Clinical Chemistry article is a narrative review, which is expert opinion rather than new data [2]. Both summaries here are based on abstracts.
