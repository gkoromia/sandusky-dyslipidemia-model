## 1.0 Summary

Lerochol (lerodalcibep-liga) is a once-monthly subcutaneous therapy approved in the United States as an adjunct to diet and exercise for adults with hypercholesterolemia, including heterozygous familial hypercholesterolemia (HeFH). Its trials showed LDL-C reductions of about 60% in studied populations, but the evidence base is LDL-C lowering rather than a Lerochol cardiovascular-outcomes trial. The practical appeal is straightforward: a self-administered monthly injection that may be stored at room temperature for up to 3 months. Whether that convenience, and the company's $199 Lerochol Direct cash-price program, improve persistence or outcomes in routine care remains unknown. [1]

## 2.0 Key Points

1. Lerochol is a monthly PCSK9-targeting injection for adults with hypercholesterolemia, including HeFH; it is used with diet and exercise. [1]
2. The Phase 3 program tested 300 mg subcutaneously every 4 weeks against placebo in adults receiving stable lipid-lowering therapy. [2]
3. The marketed LDL-C reduction of up to 60% describes lipid lowering in the trial program. It does not establish a Lerochol-specific reduction in myocardial infarction, stroke, or cardiovascular death. [1]
4. The current label lists nasopharyngitis, local injection-site reactions, and peripheral edema as adverse reactions occurring at least 2% and more often than with placebo.
5. The Lerochol Direct $199 price is a manufacturer access program, not a clinical endpoint or a universal net price.

## 3.0 What has changed

Lerochol is lerodalcibep-liga, a PCSK9-targeting recombinant fusion protein. The FDA indication is adjunctive use with diet and exercise to reduce LDL-C in adults with hypercholesterolemia, including HeFH. The recommended regimen is 300 mg by subcutaneous injection once monthly. In the Phase 3 HeFH study, the protocol compared that monthly regimen with placebo in adults already taking stable oral LDL-C-lowering therapy and needing further reduction. [1][2]

The delivery details matter. The product is available as a self-administered subcutaneous injection, and the current label permits room-temperature storage for up to 3 months. That may reduce friction for a patient who travels, has limited refrigerator space, or struggles with more frequent visits. It does not make the treatment path simpler by itself: selection, baseline therapy, response assessment, and follow-up still belong within the structured approach in {% link clinical/05-treatment-pathways.md %}.

## 4.0 What the LDL-C data show

The registration program evaluated Lerochol in randomized, placebo-controlled trials of adults with clinical ASCVD, increased ASCVD risk, or HeFH who required additional LDL-C lowering on maximally tolerated therapy. Across those settings, LDL-C lowering was approximately 60%, which is the basis for the product's "up to 60%" claim. The HeFH trial enrolled 478 adults and assessed LDL-C change at week 24 and the average of weeks 22 and 24. [1][2]

Those are meaningful LDL-C effects, but the distinction between a surrogate endpoint and a cardiovascular-outcomes result should stay explicit. The current Lerochol label refers to outcome benefits shown with statins and monoclonal-antibody PCSK9 inhibitors. It does not report a dedicated Lerochol trial showing fewer myocardial infarctions, strokes, or cardiovascular deaths. For now, the clinical case for Lerochol is its LDL-C effect and fit within a lipid-lowering regimen, not product-specific outcomes evidence.

## 5.0 Safety and practical access

In adult primary-hyperlipidemia trials, including HeFH, the adverse reactions occurring in at least 2% of Lerochol-treated patients and more often than with placebo were nasopharyngitis (15% versus 14%), local injection-site reactions (12% versus 5%), and peripheral edema (2% versus less than 1%). These label frequencies help frame counseling, but they do not replace individualized assessment of tolerability or injection technique.

LIB Therapeutics advertises Lerochol Direct at $199 with a prescription. This is potentially relevant to access, especially where coverage, prior authorization, or cost sharing obstruct treatment. It should be described accurately as a manufacturer program: terms, eligibility, dispensing, and availability may change, and a cash price does not answer the separate question of which therapy best fits a given patient.

## 6.0 Position in the treatment pathway

Lerochol expands the menu for adults who need substantial additional LDL-C lowering. The monthly self-administered regimen and room-temperature storage may be useful when they solve a real adherence or access problem. They are not substitutes for defining the LDL-C goal, documenting the response to maximally tolerated foundational therapy, or matching treatment intensity to absolute risk.

No change to the overall treatment pathway is warranted on convenience claims alone. The relevant question is whether Lerochol offers a practical way to close a patient's residual LDL-C gap after accounting for efficacy, safety, access, expected persistence, and the still-absent product-specific cardiovascular-outcomes evidence.
